Which particles become cloud droplets?

Every cloud droplet grows on a tiny particle of aerosol. Here a rising air parcel carries two separate aerosol populations, background ammonium sulphate and sea salt. As the parcel cools, the two types compete for the same water vapour. Change the air or the aerosol and watch which particles win.

What you are seeing

The parcel starts at the ground and rises at a steady speed. It cools by about 10 °C per kilometre until the vapour saturates. That point is cloud base.

Just above cloud base the air becomes slightly supersaturated, usually by less than 1 %. Every particle has a critical supersaturation that it must see before it can grow freely into a droplet. Big, soluble particles need less. The peak supersaturation decides who makes it.

Adding particles gives the vapour more places to go, so the peak falls. That is why extra sea salt can switch off some background particles.

The model

The same equations as the Fortran bin-microphysics model used in the practical, solved here in your browser:

dP/dt = −ρ g w dT/dt = (R T / cp P) dP/dt + (Lv/cp) dwl/dt dmi/dt = 4π ri D* (e∞ − eeq,i) / (RvT) eeq,i = es(T) · aw · exp(4 Mw σ / R T ρ d) dwl/dt = Σ Ni dmi/dt = −dwv/dt

Condensation only. Collision-coalescence, ice and entrainment are switched off.

Full moving bins

Each aerosol type is split into bins that hold equal numbers of particles. A bin keeps its own dry particle mass and composition for the whole run and only its water mass changes. Bins move along the size axis as they grow, so nothing is remapped and the two types never mix. That is what makes it an external mixture.

A bin counts as a cloud droplet once its water mass passes the peak of its Köhler curve. Droplet numbers are shown per cm³ of air.

Checked against the compiled Fortran model for the practical's runs: droplet numbers agree to within about 1 %.

Physics after the bin-microphysics-model by Paul Connolly, University of Manchester (EART22001 parcel modelling practical). Ventilation of falling drops is neglected, which is fine for drops smaller than about 20 µm.
